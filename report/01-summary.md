# Executive summary

A corruption complaint in Nepal can pass through nine stages. At the Commission for the Investigation of Abuse of Authority (CIAA) these are (1) the complaint, (2) screening, (3) preliminary inquiry, (4) detailed investigation and (5) the decision to prosecute. Then come (6) the Special Court trial, (7) the decision whether to appeal, (8) the Supreme Court's decision, and (9) collecting what the court orders. For each stage this research counted how many cases leave the route, how long they take, and what the public record cannot answer. Every figure comes from the institutions' own records: the CIAA's annual reports, the courts' case records, case pages and judgments, and the Supreme Court's annual reports. Every figure was recomputed by independent code before publication. Where that check changed a figure, the change is listed in the last section.

**Five findings stand out.**

1. **After a full investigation, the CIAA now closes most cases instead of prosecuting them. The change happened within two years (stages 4–5).** In FY2076/77 it decided to prosecute 441 times and closed 124 investigations without action (16.4% of its decisions). In FY2081/82 it prosecuted 137 times and closed 794 (75.1% of 1,057 decisions). The share went from 16.4% to 54.1% between FY2076/77 and FY2077/78, and it has not come back down. For the 794 closures of FY2081/82 the CIAA gives only two general grounds.

2. **Convictions often fail at the Supreme Court. Most of the convictions it overturns were bribery cases built on a method of proof that a 2021 ruling disallowed (stage 8).** When convicted people appealed, the Supreme Court fully cleared at least one of them in 230 of 567 cases (40.6%). Among cases with a published appeal judgment, the share was 83.7% where the judgment cites the April 2021 ruling on money supplied by the CIAA for a bribe, and 17.3% where it does not. *What that ruling held, and why convictions were reversed: these readings of court judgments were made with AI software and have not yet been checked by a lawyer. They may contain errors; the judgment itself is the authority.*

3. **An acquittal at the Special Court is in practice final (stages 7–8).** The government won 4 of 420 cases in which it appealed an acquittal (1.0%). For 219 of 1,010 acquittals that can be assessed (21.7%), no government appeal can be found in any record. No case number and no reason is published for any decision not to appeal.

4. **Appeals take years, and most hearing dates end with the appeal not taken up (stage 8).** For appeals registered 2008–2018, half of those decided took more than 3.8 years (4.1 years counting appeals still open). Of 27,855 hearing listings with a recorded outcome, 77.1% ended without the bench taking the appeal up. In most of these the court's own record says the bench did not reach the case, a judge could not hear it, or no bench sat.

5. **Nobody publishes how much is recovered from corruption judgments (stage 9).** The Supreme Court publishes a fine ledger every year, but for all types of case together. Leaving out the one or two courts with large one-off disposals, district courts disposed of about 11–12% of their fine ledger in each of the last two years. "Disposed" also counts prison served instead of payment, and write-offs. The only figure for government losses recovered (1.6%) comes from press reports.

**What the public record cannot answer.** At almost every stage the deciding fact is not published:
- why the CIAA closes a complaint at screening (59.6% of the 29,703 complaints it closed in FY2081/82), or why it closes a particular investigation;
- why the CIAA does not appeal a particular acquittal, why it loses the cases it loses at trial (about 4 in 10 of its cases end in full acquittal, 39.1% of 2,649 decided, and no record says how long each was investigated or what it cost), and why it leaves some people out of a charge sheet (in 12 judgments the Special Court cleared defendants partly because the CIAA had charged selectively, among them the wide-body aircraft and Pashupatinath Jalahari cases);
- which defendants in a partly upheld case were convicted. About a third of everyone prosecuted (34.3% of defendant entries in the court record) is in such a case;
- how much of what a court orders in a corruption case is ever paid.

Fourteen right-to-information requests, summarised in the last section, set out what would answer each of these.
